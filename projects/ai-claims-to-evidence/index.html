From AI Claims to Evidence

How can we tell whether claims about AI systems are credible enough to inform governance decisions?

AI developers and deployers make claims about safety, fairness, and social benefits. This project explores what evidence supports those claims, what conclusions can reasonably be drawn, and what decision-makers should do when important uncertainties remain.

THE PURPOSE

A claim is not the same as evidence, and evidence is not the same as proof.

This project is an early-stage research framework for tracing AI-related claims to supporting information - such as evaluation results, audit reports, documentation, or observed outcomes - and asking what that information actually establishes. The intended contribution is a transparent way to analyze evidence quality and its implications for oversight, not a new tool that automatically certifies AI systems.

Research case study: Can AI make hiring fairer?

One possible research direction. AI-assisted recruitment is a useful test case because AI developers (such as companies providing automated CV screening and candidate-ranking tools) may claim that their systems make hiring fairer, while applicants, employers, and regulators need to know whether the evidence supports those claims.

ILLUSTRATIVE CLAIM

“Our AI system reduces discrimination in hiring.”

What would we need to know before relying on this claim?

01 · EVIDENCE What was actually measured? Suppose a vendor reports 90% prediction accuracy and a completed bias audit. We would ask: accuracy on which applicants, against which labels, with what subgroup results, and under what testing conditions? A performance score alone does not establish fairer hiring.
02 · INFERENCE Does the evidence support the claim? Were error rates and selection outcomes compared across relevant groups? Is the sample representative of actual applicants? To claim the system reduces discrimination, what credible comparison with hiring without the system is available? Association, prediction, and causal impact are different.
03 · GOVERNANCE What should decision-makers do? Should an employer request more documentation, commission an independent audit, pilot the tool with safeguards, monitor outcomes, or avoid deployment until the evidence improves? The evidentiary standard should match the decision and stakes.


Why this matters in practice: Governments are already confronting these questions.

  • In the UK, official guidance discusses evidence of bias audits, while the Information Commissioner’s Office has investigated AI recruitment tools.

  • In the US, New York City’s Local Law 144 requires bias audits and public summaries of their results for covered automated employment decision tools.

  • Meanwhile, the EU AI Act classifies certain AI systems used in recruitment as high-risk, establishing additional requirements for their providers and deployers.

These examples raise a common research question: What evidence is sufficient to demonstrate that an AI system is fair, reliable, and suitable for real-world use?

Potential research output:

  • A comparative study of publicly documented claims about fairness in AI-assisted recruitment, examining the evidence used to support them, the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn, and the implications for governance.

  • The study could produce a structured evidence matrix and a policy paper identifying gaps in current evaluation practices and recommending ways to improve transparency, accountability, and regulatory oversight.

How the Research Project Approaches the Problem

This project is built around three interconnected questions: how to assess the credibility of evidence about AI systems, determine what conclusions it supports, and translate those findings into informed governance decisions.

01EVIDENCE QUALITY

What makes AI governance evidence credible?

How We Investigate These Questions

This project combines policy research, comparative case studies, and quantitative analysis to examine how AI-related claims are supported by evidence and how that evidence can inform governance decisions.

METHOD 01

Map evidence requirements

Compare public evaluation, assurance, and governance approaches; identify which decisions they support and what information they require.

METHOD 02

Analyze selected cases

Trace claims to underlying evidence in a small, transparent sample; code gaps, uncertainty, incentives, and limitations. Interviews are optional and subject to access.

METHOD 03

Test decision implications

Develop a decision-focused comparison of evidence thresholds or review triggers; use quantitative analysis only where justified by the available evidence.

Research integrity and limits

A model-generated summary is not independent verification. An audit is not necessarily evidence of real-world safety, and an observed association is not necessarily a causal effect. This concept calls for traceable sources, explicit assumptions, consideration of affected parties, and clear separation between empirical findings and normative judgments. No evidence collection, interviews, model evaluations, or empirical validation have been completed for this concept.

From Research to Policy Recommendations

EXPECTED CONTRIBUTION

Central question: What evidence should decision-makers require before relying on claims about AI systems?

This project aims to translate research into actionable insights for AI governance. An initial contribution could be a comparative study of publicly documented AI evaluation and assurance practices, examining the credibility of supporting evidence, identifying gaps and limitations, and assessing their implications for governance decisions.

The research could produce a policy paper or research report, accompanied by a structured evidence matrix that documents the supporting evidence, its limitations, and the conclusions it can reasonably support. Together, these outputs would offer practical recommendations for strengthening transparency, accountability, and regulatory oversight.
